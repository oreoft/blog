## Preface

Q: Why does e-waste so often get a second life?  
A: Hackintosh.

My very first personal computer was a 2015 MacBook Pro. As the last MBP with the iconic glowing Apple logo, it still holds its value well, and plenty of power users are still using it today. The usability of macOS, its support for high-DPI displays, a software environment that's a bit less shady, and the incredibly strong ecosystem have always had a firm grip on me. I’d been eyeing last year’s 16-inch model for a long time, so I kept feeling like my current machine was starting to run out of steam. But with the high price, my not-so-high budget, plus the temptation to wait for ARM at the end of the year and Tiger Lake next year... well, the “wait-and-see” crowd never really loses.

Sure enough, the one who understands me best is always Zhang Damei. Recently there were a few M73 Hackintosh posts on SMZDM, and they were just too good. The Lenovo M73, a 1L mini PC from years ago, can do so much for just a bit over 200 RMB. That instantly made me dig those videos back up and review them properly. After watching, my scavenger-builder spirit reignited, and I decided to build one myself. I really hadn’t experienced what macOS felt like on a standard-voltage CPU before. I’d previously messed around with Hackintosh on a NUC8i5 and a T470, but the experience was pretty average. I’d followed Xiao Bing’s blog, lurked around PCEVA, and had even seen Sibotu’s M73 video before, but I didn’t pay much attention to it at the time.

The reason I ignored it back then was that I used to firmly believe in “buy new, not old,” always trying to ride the wave of the times. Then I discovered Xianyu and became a junk hardware guy... but that’s another story. Later, though, I realized my work computer had a 4th-gen i5-4590, and surprisingly, it was really good. I don’t know whether Intel has just always been this slippery, or whether the workloads I use daily simply don’t need that much horsepower. After doing a lot of research and comparisons, I found that aside from Intel’s years of incremental “toothpaste squeezing,” the 4th-gen Haswell architecture actually isn’t that far behind modern Coffee Lake in single-core performance. The gap is more obvious in multi-threading and power consumption.

So I wrote this article as the first post in my tinkering section, just to document the process. Since I’ve been playing with Hackintosh for quite a while, this article leans a bit more toward unboxing and tutorial content. Hopefully it can also give some ideas to others who want to tinker with the same thing. All the software tools used in this article are provided at the end, and you can jump there directly from the table of contents on the right. The EFI I used is based on Sibotu’s third version. Honestly, his work was already very complete. I only changed three things: 1. Set the VRAM size to 2048MB to reduce graphical glitches. 2. Added speaker support for the M73. 3. Disabled verbose boot mode.

This is the Wi-Fi card. At 100 RMB, it was basically free. The condition was so good I suspect it was also first-hand, unlike the flashy label-covered monsters all over Xianyu. There are lots of tutorials online claiming to teach you how to build a “perfect Hackintosh,” and what they usually mean by “perfect” is that AirDrop works. That makes sense, because AirDrop is one of the signature features of the Apple ecosystem—file transfer between devices, shared clipboard across devices, iPad Sidecar, and a whole bunch of really practical features all build on top of that foundation. But the requirements are extremely strict. Ordinary Wi-Fi cards usually can’t achieve this, because Apple only uses Broadcom Wi-Fi cards, and Broadcom cards are OEM-only—they’re not sold directly to regular consumers... So the best solution is to use a pulled card, like one salvaged from a broken Apple computer and resold. They’re expensive, but it’s still the best option. And now Apple’s hardware control is getting stricter and stricter—for example, using the T2 chip to secure hardware, and soldering the Wi-Fi card directly onto the motherboard. Every pulled card used is one fewer left in the world. So what are you waiting for?

## Creating the Boot Image

The core of a Hackintosh is the EFI, not the boot image or the system image. So I didn’t follow the common approach of pre-configuring all kinds of EFI parameters in advance like many current guides do. Most people try to do everything in one shot, which raises the barrier too high and scares off beginners. My approach is to first use Sibotu’s default EFI, then after entering the system, use the eternal god of Hackintosh tools—Hackintool—to adjust the parameters. My philosophy is that the important stuff should be tuned carefully at the end. So I simplified the installation into just two steps.

1. **For the boot image, I used one that had already been downloaded offline, and wrote it to my USB drive using the "imageUSB" software, then booted into the installer from the USB drive.**

   1. Download and open the resource pack, then open the first folder inside it - Create Boot USB. There are two files inside: `imageUSB.exe`, which is the tool used to write the USB drive, and `bootloader.img`, which is the boot image modified for the M73 based on Apple’s offline download.

   ![image-20200929115012282](https://mypicgogo.oss-cn-hangzhou.aliyuncs.com/tuchuang/20200929115012.png)
   <center>The resource pack will be provided at the end of the article; this is the first folder in it</center><br/>

   2. Open `imageUSB.exe`, then in the big box in step 1, select your USB drive. In step 3, specify the `bootloader.img` mentioned above. Leave everything else unchanged, then click `write`.

   ![image-20200929115431485](https://mypicgogo.oss-cn-hangzhou.aliyuncs.com/tuchuang/20200929115431.png)
   <center>Click write to start flashing the image</center><br/>

   3. Wait for the progress bar to finish. Then check the USB drive you just wrote to—you’ll see its volume name has changed to `BOOT`. Open the USB drive and you’ll find only one `EFI` folder inside (there’s actually also a hidden folder). At this point, the USB drive is already capable of booting our EFI. But as mentioned above, the core of Hackintosh is the EFI, and future upgrades or iterations mainly involve replacing EFI files. So to make sure the EFI is the latest version, we’ll replace it once more. The replacement file is in the second folder of the resource pack provided in this article - `2.EFI` (please copy the `EFI` folder inside). Just like the folder name says, copy the `EFI` folder inside and replace the one under the `BOOT` volume.

   ![image-20200929143305](https://mypicgogo.oss-cn-hangzhou.aliyuncs.com/tuchuang/20200929143305.png)
   
   <center>Replace the EFI</center><br/>

At this point, the bootable USB drive is ready, and next we’ll use it to boot the M73 into macOS installation. A lot of people might be wondering right now: That’s it? That’s it? That’s it? Other people’s guides are full of long, complicated steps. In reality, I didn’t skip a single step—I just used a simpler way to achieve the same result. Of course, there are tradeoffs. For example, in Sibotu’s guide, you need to install Python and run the scripts he provides. His scripts download the macOS recovery image from Apple’s official servers, then you have to go through a more complicated process to choose the version info, and then the script writes the image to the USB drive and adjusts the EFI afterward. That whole process is really discouraging for beginners. Downloading the image offline and writing it with software is much simpler, but the downside is that you can’t dynamically fetch the recovery image version. It really just depends on what you prefer. My suggestion is to first achieve the goal with the simplest method, then spend time later understanding the underlying principles.

2. **The system image itself is downloaded and installed online from Apple’s official servers by the recovery program in the installer interface.**

Insert the USB drive you just created, press that weirdly satisfying power button, and let’s begin the system installation. On the POST screen, press `F12` once to enter the boot device selection menu, and choose the bootable USB drive we made. After entering, the first screen shown by the USB bootloader will appear. On this screen, choose the option for entering the boot USB functions—we want the third one, which is Apple’s official system recovery mode. Through this recovery mode, we’ll install macOS 10.15.6 online onto the computer. Use the keyboard up/down arrows and Enter to move and confirm. If you don’t make any selection within 3 seconds, it will automatically boot into the first option by default. Since we need the third one, you have to act quickly. If you want to study the screen more carefully, just press any key to interrupt the auto-boot. After selecting it, some related code will run, and you may see lots of log messages you don’t understand. Don’t worry about them—just wait.

At this point, macOS is installed on the M73. The steps here are probably the simplest you’ll find in public guides. Honestly, after generations of effort from the community, installing a Hackintosh is now even easier than installing Windows in some ways. Once the boot drive is ready, the rest is mostly handled by Apple’s own tools. But the real key is compatibility—after the system is installed, how do you minimize the rejection between your hardware and macOS? Aside from choosing hardware as close as possible to real Macs, the rest is endless adaptation, spoofing, and trial and error. After all, not every machine is as blessed as the NUC8. So next, we’ll enter the system, get rid of USB booting, spoof a few incompatible hardware components, and generate SMBIOS values so we can sign in to Apple ID.

## Summary

After countless forced shutdowns, countless image writes, and endless EFI modifications and replacements, I finally got Hackintosh running on the M73. I have to say, a standard-voltage CPU really feels amazing. I originally thought the fan wouldn’t be able to handle the heat, but in reality, the fan is extremely quiet—you can only hear it if you get close—yet it can sustain a 40W output, which really surprised me.

Overall, it’s pretty close to perfect. I didn’t run into any software issues, and the experience feels very similar to my real Mac. The only issue is that wake-from-sleep sometimes fails, but after enabling TRIM on the SSD, it got much better. Now it’s just a slight delay. There are actually many more software-side and BIOS-side tuning steps that I didn’t explain in detail due to length. If I come across another machine like this that’s just too good to pass up, I’ll definitely share it again.

In the end, even though Hackintosh has all sorts of little issues, that doesn’t stop me from loving it. Sure, it’s a lot of tinkering, but when you fully throw yourself into something, slowly moving forward through repeated mistakes, the sense of achievement when you finally succeed is more than enough to keep me hooked.
