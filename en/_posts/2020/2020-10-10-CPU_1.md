## Preface

On 2020.10.8 (local time), AMD released its new generation of Zen 3 processors, which sparked a lot of discussion. AMD had been suppressed by Intel for a long time, but in recent years it doubled its market share thanks to its “Yes” energy. This time, AMD officially claimed a 19% IPC uplift with Zen 3, which really felt like rubbing Intel’s face into the ground. In the past, AMD usually competed by stacking more cores and going for the “big bowl, great value” style, but this time its single-core performance even surpassed the i9-10900K for the first time, beating Intel by 16% in Cinebench R20. Sure, the price went up a bit, and the once dragon-slaying boy has now become the new dragon. But with rumors saying Intel’s 11th-gen desktop processors would still use the 14nm+++ process, people still couldn’t help shouting: “AMD Yes!”

With AMD’s new processors launching, I figured I’d summarize the current categories of consumer CPUs. On both desktop and mobile platforms, the two major processor vendors keep releasing new products every year, and their product lines can get a little confusing for ordinary consumers. So I put together this summary to make it easier for everyone to shop and learn.

![image-20201011144544630](https://mypicgogo.oss-cn-hangzhou.aliyuncs.com/tuchuang/20201011144544.png)

First, let me quote Baidu Baike’s introduction to CPUs:

> The central processing unit (CPU, Central Processing Unit) is an ultra-large-scale integrated circuit and serves as both the computing core (Core) and control core (Control Unit) of a computer. Its main functions are to interpret computer instructions and process data in computer software. A CPU mainly includes the arithmetic unit (Arithmetic Logic Unit, ALU), cache memory (Cache), and the buses (Bus) for data, control, and status that connect them. Together with internal memory (Memory) and input/output (I/O) devices, it forms the three core components of an electronic computer.

When it comes to consumer PC CPUs, people are basically choosing directly between **AMD** and **Intel**. A chip is really like a giant maze, with tens of billions of branching paths inside and only a handful of exits. From an engineering perspective, that’s basically like building a miniature city of Wuhan in the microscopic world, while also making sure the city’s “infrastructure” runs in an orderly way: what should go in goes in, what should go out goes out, and internal “public security” is fully guaranteed. Every “road” and every “building” has to survive trillions of passes without any issues. A chip that meets spec has no real concept of “repair,” because its lifespan doesn’t depend on how frequently you use it, but on the limits of fundamental physics and quantum mechanics. In the traditional consumer mindset, it’s basically something that “just doesn’t break.” The reason there are so few top-tier processor manufacturers is that every single chip you buy has to satisfy all of the above conditions, and that requires extremely deep technical accumulation and massive capital investment.

## Intel’s Blue Camp

Intel is currently the processor manufacturer with the highest market share in consumer desktops and mobile devices (for now). As a world-leading CPU company, Intel’s blue logo always gives off a premium vibe. Intel is also nicknamed the “toothpaste factory,” because it dominated the field so completely in its early years that there was basically no real competition, which led to very slow iteration. It kept squeezing the same old process for years, and in some aspects there hasn’t been a leap in performance from 4th-gen processors to today’s 10th-gen ones.

### Common Series

Pentium

> Pentium was once Intel’s glory days. I still remember Intel’s old ad jingle — “deng deng deng deng deng” — “give your computer a Pentium heart.” Pure nostalgia. Although advances in process technology and rising consumer spending led Intel to launch the Core series in 2006, pushing Pentium off its pedestal, the “Pentium” brand was never abandoned. As a familiar consumer brand, it gradually shifted toward budget products. But these days, even when people are really squeezing their budget for a PC build, they’ll usually grit their teeth and go for a Core chip instead of choosing Pentium.
> ![image-20201012100316265](https://mypicgogo.oss-cn-hangzhou.aliyuncs.com/tuchuang/20201012100316.png)

Celeron

> A processor line positioned slightly below Pentium. There’s also an OEM market for it, where it’s commonly used in mini PCs and set-top boxes. On Xianyu, hardware scavengers often use it for soft routers.... But ordinary consumers rarely use it. You might run into it when building a NAS or a soft router, but for normal office work this series is not commonly chosen.

Core

> This is Intel’s current flagship mainstream series, and also the one most people choose. Core is the familiar i-series, divided into i3, i5, i7, and i9, with the X series beyond that. Core count, clock speed, and performance all increase step by step. It’s split into desktop and mobile platforms. At present, the desktop platform has reached the 10th generation, while the mobile platform has reached the 11th generation.
>
> ![img](https://pic4.zhimg.com/80/v2-8b321e95c0931612c05c204ca1d14e83_1440w.jpg?source=1940ef5c)

Atom

> This was the series Intel originally launched to grab a share of the smartphone chip market. What left the deepest impression on me was Lenovo’s K900, released in 2013 with the Atom Z2580. It had a silver, sharp-edged design, a dual-core quad-thread CPU at 2GHz, 2GB of RAM, and a 13MP camera — pretty stunning at the time. They even got Kobe Bryant to endorse it.
>
> Later, the mobile chip market got way too fierce, and Atom insisted on using x86 architecture, which made developer adaptation harder. Market response was mediocre. At the end of April 2016, two new versions of the Atom mobile processor line that had originally been planned for release in 2016 were canceled, and Intel exited the smartphone chip market. It’s now discontinued.
>
> ![img](https://timgsa.baidu.com/timg?image&quality=80&size=b9999_10000&sec=1602402584560&di=9be54c477d32101f37e5b8cc53f795f0&imgtype=0&src=http%3A%2F%2Fimg3.imgtn.bdimg.com%2Fit%2Fu%3D3964826118%2C182233367%26fm%3D214%26gp%3D0.jpg)

Xeon

> This is Intel’s processor line for the server market. Wait, wasn’t this supposed to be about consumer-grade CPUs? Why are we talking about servers now? Actually, it’s because early Xeons had absurdly good price/performance, and they used the same LGA 1150 socket, which meant even the lowest-end E3 models performed on par with Core i7 chips of the same era. The legendary “god-tier CPU” was the E3-1231 V3: Core i5 price, Core i7 performance — insanely good. But today’s Xeons are truly Xeons: there are obvious gaps from the Core series in price, performance, and stability, and the lineup has become more vertically segmented into Xeon E, Xeon W, Xeon, and so on. Still, those early E-series chips are still circulating in the market, because Intel kept squeezing toothpaste....

The first one or two digits indicate the generation (because we’re already at the 11th generation now, so it’s gone into double digits).

The bigger the middle number, the stronger it is.

The last two may be absent; if present, they’re usually a letter suffix.

### Suffix Buffs

| Suffix | Meaning |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| B     | Desktop soldered standard-voltage version |
| F     | No integrated graphics |
| G     | Integrated graphics from AMD |
| H     | Soldered standard-voltage laptop version |
| S     | Special edition |
| K     | Unlocked multiplier, can be overclocked |
| Q     | Means quad, four cores |
| M     | Dual-core low-voltage mobile, commonly used before 4th gen |
| Y     | Ultra-low-voltage mobile, very common back when ultrabooks were popular |
| T     | Lower-voltage desktop version, slightly below standard voltage |
| U     | Low-voltage mobile version, basically all modern business ultrabooks use U-series |
| X/XE  | HEDT (high-end desktop) processors, usually with a very high core count and quad-channel memory support; all specs are far beyond ordinary PCs |
| G1-G7 | Intel’s new naming method for the 11th generation; G emphasizes graphics performance, and the higher the number, the stronger it is |

## AMD’s Red Camp

AMD helped Intel redesign product pricing in its early days, and the two once traded blows pretty evenly. But later, AMD fell far behind Intel in process technology. By the time Intel had already entered the 22nm era, AMD was still polishing 32nm. For many years it was pinned under Intel’s feet, and because of its low prices, it became the go-to trap card for shady PC shop owners scamming beginners. But AMD has always fought on two fronts — CPUs against Intel and GPUs against NVIDIA. Now AMD’s CPUs are manufactured by TSMC, letting it jump straight into the 7nm process era, and its RX graphics cards are still capable of going toe-to-toe with NVIDIA. I’m really looking forward to the future of the consumer electronics market.

### Series

Athlon

> One of AMD’s early flagship brands, mainly known for being cheap. K8 was once a main force before being overshadowed by other series, but because of its low price it won the favor of many consumers.

“Rural Enterprise” Series

> This is actually a nickname for some of the architectures AMD used during its development, such as Piledriver, Steamroller, and Excavator. Because the names were so distinctive and the prices were so low, people loved calling AMD a “rural enterprise.” And it was through constantly refining this so-called rural enterprise series that AMD eventually arrived at Ryzen with the Zen architecture. During that period, the tempting prices won over wave after wave of loyal fans.

Ryzen

> This is AMD at its peak right now, directly targeting Intel’s popular consumer Core series. The most noteworthy thing is that AMD’s share in the mobile consumer market doubled almost entirely thanks to Ryzen. Even the proud and aloof ThinkPad released 3500U versions, which for a while became entry-level “so worth it” machines. Then this year the 4000 series kicked off another wave of hype, with the 4800U pushing mobile processors all the way to 8 cores and 16 threads. Keep in mind, the famous toothpaste factory used dual-core mobile processors for more than a decade, and it wasn’t until 2018’s 8th-gen Core that Intel finally gave us quad-core. The Ryzen series is categorized with an R prefix, similar to Intel’s i-series: R3, R5, R7, and R9, with core count, clock speed, and performance increasing step by step. One especially notable chip is the sold-out 4800H. With a 45W standard-voltage 8-core 16-thread design plus Vega integrated graphics, it was cheaper than Intel laptops using 10th-gen Core i5 chips, while its performance absolutely rubbed 10th-gen Core i7 into the ground. At the time, the strongest 10th-gen Core i7 only had up to 6 cores, which forced Intel to launch the 8-core 10875H in response — and that still got hammered. The 4800H’s monster specs won over a huge number of fans, which led to supply shortages, and then the price went up and it turned into a dragon 😬
>
> ![image-20201011144031327](https://mypicgogo.oss-cn-hangzhou.aliyuncs.com/tuchuang/20201011144031.png)

Threadripper

> This is AMD’s HEDT platform processor line launched in recent years, aimed at Intel’s X/XE suffix processors. The latest at the moment is the 3rd-gen 3000 series released in 2019, with 64 cores and 128 threads. Hearing that is shocking, but also gives you the feeling of “wow, but I’ll never use that.” And honestly, for ordinary PCs — gaming, office work, and so on — there’s absolutely no need to consider the HEDT platform unless you just won 10 million and have no idea how else to spend the money. That said, HEDT platforms are extremely suitable for productivity workloads that heavily depend on threads and cores, such as video editing and 3D modeling/rendering. For ordinary consumers, the significance of AMD Threadripper and Intel X/XE is basically this: they’re using these insanely powerful performance monsters as brand endorsements — “Look, we can make CPUs this powerful, so come buy our chips.”
>
> ![img](http://www.lotpc.com/uploads/allimg/200404/1-2004041Z4335T.jpg)

EPYC

> This is AMD’s latest server processor line, going up against Intel’s Xeon. Performance is very strong, and power consumption is also pretty scary at 200W+. I can’t afford it and don’t need it, so I can only admire it from afar and hear that its compute power is amazing. But personally, I think that outside of supercomputers, servers should still prioritize stability. A lot of standards were set by Intel, and Intel also has many patented acceleration technologies. So maybe AMD still has a long road ahead if it wants to truly break into the server market.

### Model Numbers

xxxxxx — the first four digits are numbers, followed by two optional suffix letters.

The first digit indicates the Zen architecture generation. Originally, desktop and mobile versions differed by 1, but starting from the 5th generation AMD unified them to keep things consistent. The latest ones currently start with 5.

Starting from the second digit, bigger means stronger, with 9 being the strongest.

The third digit is usually 0, except for Threadripper and the Ryzen 3950X.

The fourth digit is currently 0 for all models.

### Suffix Buffs

| Suffix | Meaning |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| E    | Low power, roughly equivalent to Intel’s T |
| X    | Supports XFR dynamic frequency scaling, roughly equivalent to Intel’s K (though AMD’s whole lineup is now multiplier-unlocked anyway) |
| U    | Low-voltage laptop version, same as Intel |
| H    | Standard-voltage laptop version, same as Intel |
| G    | Means it has integrated graphics; without it, there is no integrated graphics by default, the opposite of Intel’s F |
| T    | Means Turbo support, similar to Intel’s Turbo Boost |

## Ending

This is just an introductory article, only briefly covering the product lines of Intel and AMD. If you’re a beginner learning to build your own PC and don’t want to spend too much time on it, then my suggestion is simple: within the price range you can afford, buy the most expensive one. After all, if you know quality, how could money not know quality too?
